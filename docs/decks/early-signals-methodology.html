Early Signals · approach and method

Can routine records point us to gastric cancer sooner?

A way to read the clinic visits, blood counts and prescriptions we already collect, and find the few patients who most need an endoscopy.

The idea

Watch, score, act. The doctor always decides.

1

Watch

Follow everyone who has come to a clinic with a stomach complaint.

Visits, symptoms, blood counts, weight, prescriptions, H. pylori tests.

2

Score

Every month, estimate each patient's chance of a gastric cancer diagnosis in the next year.

Each score comes with its reasons in plain words.

3

Act

Put the highest-risk patients on the endoscopy list; test and treat H. pylori; recheck falling Hb.

A doctor reviews every flag. Nothing reaches a patient without a doctor's approval.

  • This is decision support, a smarter referral list. It does not diagnose.
  • Scopes are scarce: the goal is to use each one on the patient most likely to benefit.

Widths are not to scale.

A patient joins the GI cohort at the first of

  • a diagnosis of gastritis, ulcer, dyspepsia, reflux, GI bleed or H. pylori
  • 2 or more visits in 12 months with a stomach complaint
  • any H. pylori test, or any endoscopy
  • iron-deficiency anaemia at age 40 or over

Why top 2%? The cut-off is set by endoscopy capacity: the list should fit next month's scope slots.

  • We do not score the whole population: only people already touching the system for stomach problems.
  • The 2% is a capacity choice. We would set it with you, from the real number of scopes per month.

The exact question

"Using only what is recorded up to today, how likely is a gastric cancer diagnosis in the next 1–12 months?"

The 30-day gap

Stops the model from "predicting" cancers whose work-up has already started.

Never used as inputs

Endoscopy, biopsy, pathology, staging, CT abdomen, CEA, or a "suspected gastric malignancy" code. These are the diagnosis itself; the model must work before them.

  • Same logic as a "landmark" analysis in survival studies: a reference date, only the past is visible.
  • We test this automatically: removing all post-landmark data never changes a score.

Present for years

D tells us who is at risk. C, B and A tell us that something is changing now. We read them in that order, as a story.

  • Mnemonic for the meeting: A for Alarm, B for Blood, C for Coming back, D for Drivers.
  • D is the soil; C, B, A are the weather changing. Ask which group they think matters most locally.

B · Blood

A slow bleed shows up as haemoglobin sliding against the patient's own baseline

Haemoglobin (g/dL) before diagnosis

Illustrative · in the simulation ~60% of cases have a falling Hb, visible only if a blood count was done

What we read from the record

  • Last Hb, lowest Hb, and the trend (g/dL per month) over 12 months
  • Drop from the patient's own best value in 12 months
  • Anaemia by sex-specific cut-off; iron prescribed
+3

Hb fell ≥ 1.5 g/dL in 12 months, or anaemia with no other cause recorded

Rwanda-specific trap: anaemia labelled as malaria or worms in the last 12 months. We record it as its own signal, because it can delay the work-up by months.

  • Personal baseline matters more than a fixed threshold: 12.5 can be normal for one patient and a 2-point drop for another.
  • Follow-up action: iron, recheck Hb at 4 weeks, scope if still low or falling.
  • Ask: how often is a full blood count done at health-centre level?

C · Coming back

The pattern across visits: more frequent, and the medicine is not working

What we read from the record

  • Stomach-complaint visits in the last 6, 12 and 24 months
  • Speeding up: visits in the last 6 months minus the 6 before
  • Days since the last stomach-complaint visit
  • PPI and antacid courses; "no resolution" = 2+ PPI courses and the patient came back again

How the signals evolve

Signals switch on in order. That order is the window to act.

Illustrative curves from the simulation's design: share of future cases with each signal in the trailing 12 months
  • Drivers are flat: they were there all along. "Coming back" rises first, then blood, then alarm symptoms in the last months.
  • Grey dashed line: matched patients who did not develop cancer. The gap between them is what the model uses.
  • In the simulation the median time from the first stomach complaint to diagnosis is 7–10 months. Ask: does this match what they see?

Specific signals trigger their own pathway, at any risk level

Alarm, no scope in 90 daysEndoscopy within 14 days
Haemoglobin dropIron → recheck Hb at 4 weeks → scope if still low or falling

Follow-through: app reminder → SMS on the due date → community health worker visit after 7 days → back to the doctor after 14. Patient messages advise a visit only and never name a diagnosis.

  • The score is not the end product; the care plan is. Every plan is approved by a doctor in the app.
  • Pathway timings follow Maastricht VI (test of cure) and common anaemia practice; they are demo defaults to tune with clinicians.

The models

Three ways to score, from simple to sophisticated

Tier 1

The checklist

10 rules, points added up. Like a clinical score you could do on paper.

Written by hand from the evidence, not fitted to the data. Fully transparent.

Tier 2

The pattern learner

Hundreds of small decision trees (XGBoost), each correcting the last. Learns combinations, e.g. falling Hb and repeat visits.

Explains every flag with its top 5 reasons (SHAP).

Tier 3

The timeline reader

A sequence model reads the last 36 months of events in order, with their timing, like a clinician reading the chart.

Highlights the timeline events that mattered most.

Final score = average of the pattern learner and the timeline reader, calibrated so that "20%" means about 1 in 5. The checklist stays visible as a sanity check.

  • Why three: the checklist is easy to trust and teach; the learners catch combinations and timing the checklist cannot.
  • If the learners disagree with the checklist on a patient, that is worth a look.

Checks and results

How we keep it honest, and what it scored on synthetic data

Tested on the future

Trained on 2016–mid 2023; tested on mid 2023–2025, on patients it never saw.

No peeking

Automatic leakage tests. With labels shuffled, the score falls to chance (0.50).

Negative control

HIV has no effect by design, and the analysis finds none.

Real-world framing

Calibration, lead time, and number of scopes needed per cancer found.

Ranking accuracy (AUROC) on the test period

Read with care: synthetic patients are cleaner than real ones. Published models on real records score lower, and we expect that here too. Real data is the true test.

  • AUROC: 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect ranking.
  • Why so high: in synthetic data, future cases come back monthly while most cohort members have not been seen for years. That makes ranking easy. We report it as measured and flag it.

Next step

A first real-data check: look back from confirmed cases

  1. List the cases

    Confirmed gastric cancers diagnosed in recent years, from pathology and oncology records.

  2. Pick matched patients

    About 5 per case: same sex, age ± 5 years, province and period, with stomach complaints but no cancer.

  3. Look back 24 months

    Pull visits, labs, weights and prescriptions from before each diagnosis date.

  4. Compare A, B, C, D

    How often each signal appears in cases vs matched patients, and how early.

  5. Score and compare

    Run the checklist, then retrain the learners. Compare to the simulation.

Success looks like

Signals clearly more common in cases; flags 3+ months before diagnosis.

Safeguards

Ethics approval, de-identified extracts, no patient contact.
